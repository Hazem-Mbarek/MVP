QUESTION:
CMR Article 17 discusses carrier liability. What specific circumstances can relieve the carrier of responsibility, and what about the special risks listed in Article 17(4)?

ANSWER:
**CMR Article 17 – Carrier liability**

**1. Circumstances that relieve the carrier of responsibility (Article 17 para 2)**  
The carrier is **not liable** for loss, damage or delay when it is caused by any of the following factors (CMR‑17‑2)【CMR-17-2, cmr】:

| Reason | Explanation |
|--------|--------------|
| **Wrongful act or neglect of the claimant** | The claimant’s own misconduct caused the loss/damage. |
| **Instructions of the claimant** (when not the result of the carrier’s wrongful act) | The carrier acted on the claimant’s directions that led to the loss/damage. |
| **Inherent vice of the goods** | The goods themselves have a natural defect that caused the loss/damage. |
| **Circumstances beyond the carrier’s control that could not be avoided or their consequences prevented** | Events the carrier could not have foreseen or avoided (e.g., natural disasters, acts of third parties). |

**2. Special risks listed in Article 17 para 4**  
When loss, damage or delay is attributable to any of the “special risks” enumerated in paragraph 4, the following rules apply (CMR‑18‑2)【CMR-18-2, cmr】:

| Special risk (para 4) | Effect on liability |
|------------------------|----------------------|
| **(a) Accident, act of public authority, or act of war** | Presumption that the loss/damage is caused by the risk, unless the claimant proves otherwise. |
| **(b) Fire, explosion, or other external causes** | Same presumption; claimant may rebut it. |
| **(c) Shortage, loss of any package, or abnormal shortage** | Presumption applies, but **does not apply** if there has been an abnormal shortage or loss of any package (CMR‑18‑3)【CMR-18-3, cmr】. |
| **(d) Effects of heat, cold, temperature variations, or humidity** – when goods are carried in specially equipped vehicles | Carrier cannot claim the benefit of para 4(d) unless it proves it took all required steps (choice, maintenance, use of equipment) and complied with any special instructions (CMR‑18‑5)【CMR-18-5, cmr】. |
| **(f) Any other risk expressly listed in the Convention** | Carrier must prove it fulfilled all normal obligations and complied with any special instructions before invoking relief (CMR‑18‑5)【CMR-18-5, cmr】. |

**Key points**

* The **burden of proof** that loss/damage falls under one of these special risks lies with the **carrier** (CMR‑18‑1)【CMR-18-1, cmr】.  
* Once the carrier establishes that a special risk applies, a **presumption of liability** is created, but the **claimant may rebut** it by proving the loss/damage was not caused (in whole or in part) by that risk.  

**Summary**  
The carrier is exempt from liability when loss/damage results from the claimant’s fault, the claimant’s instructions, inherent vice of the goods, or unavoidable external circumstances. For the special risks in Article 17 para 4, liability is presumed unless the carrier cannot meet the specific proof and compliance requirements, and the claimant can still challenge the presumption.

QUESTION:
Under CMR, what role does the consignment note play as evidence? Can a carrier dispute the contents of a properly completed consignment note?

ANSWER:
The CMR Convention states that the consignment note is **prima facie evidence of the making of the contract of carriage, the conditions of that contract and the carrier’s receipt of the goods**【CMR-9-1, cmr】.  

If the consignment note **contains no specific reservations by the carrier**, it is presumed—unless the carrier can prove otherwise—that the goods and their packaging were in good condition when taken over and that the number, marks and description of the packages correspond to what is recorded in the note【CMR-9-2, cmr】.  

Therefore, a carrier may only dispute the contents of a properly completed consignment note by **making a specific reservation** in the note or by **providing proof that contradicts the presumption**; otherwise the note’s statements are taken as evidence and cannot be contested.

QUESTION:
A client is shipping fragile electronics DDP via road. At what point do we assume liability under CMR? What happens if damage occurs in transit vs. after delivery?

ANSWER:
**Liability under the CMR (road carriage)**  

- The carrier becomes liable **from the moment it takes over the goods until the moment of delivery**.  This covers any total or partial loss, damage, or delay that occurs while the carrier is in possession of the goods. {CMR‑17‑1, cmr}  

**If damage occurs in transit**  

- Because the carrier’s liability extends throughout the period it holds the goods, any damage that happens while the goods are being transported is the carrier’s responsibility. {CMR‑17‑1, cmr}  

**If damage occurs after delivery**  

- Once the goods have been delivered to the consignee, the carrier’s liability under the CMR ends.  Any damage that appears after delivery is no longer covered by the carrier’s CMR liability. {CMR‑17‑1, cmr}  

*(No other source provides additional CMR‑specific rules for post‑delivery damage, so the answer is limited to the liability period defined in Article 17‑1.)*

QUESTION:
A shipper claims we damaged goods on a CIP shipment, but the goods were improperly packaged. Under CMR, can we defend ourselves? What does the consignment note evidence?

ANSWER:
**Defence under the CMR**

- The CMR places the carrier’s liability for loss or damage on the period “from the time when he takes over the goods … to the time of delivery”【CMR-17-1, cmr】.  
- However, the carrier may escape liability if it can show that the damage was caused by **defective or insufficient packaging**. Article 17‑4 b states that “the lack of, or defective condition of packing …” is a recognised ground for limiting the carrier’s responsibility【CMR-17-4b, cmr】.  

Therefore, you can defend the claim by proving that the goods were **improperly packaged** before the carrier took them over.

**What the consignment note proves**

- If the consignment note contains **no specific reservations** made by the carrier, the CMR presumes, unless the contrary is proved, that *the goods and their packaging appeared to be in good condition* when the carrier received them, and that the *number of packages, their marks and numbers corresponded with the statements in the consignment note*【CMR-9-2, cmr】.  
- The note also records the **apparent condition of the goods and their packaging**【CMR-8-1b, cmr】, which can be used as evidence of the state of the goods at the moment of taking‑over.  

Consequently, the consignment note serves as a **prima facie indication** that the carrier received the goods in satisfactory condition, shifting the burden to the shipper (or the carrier) to demonstrate that the packaging was defective. If you can produce evidence (e.g., photographs, packing inspection reports) showing the packaging was inadequate, the presumption created by the consignment note can be rebutted, supporting your defence.

Therefore, **CIF shifts more responsibility to the seller** because it adds the obligation to procure insurance for the shipment, whereas DAP leaves insurance to the buyer.

QUESTION:
A shipper packs goods inadequately for a CIP sea journey. The goods are damaged en route. Under CMR principles (if this were road), would the shipper or carrier be liable? Explain the logic.

ANSWER:
Under the CMR Convention the carrier is **normally liable** for any loss or damage that occurs after he has taken over the goods and before delivery — see Article 17 (1) which states that “the carrier shall be liable for the total or partial loss of the goods and for damage thereto occurring between the time when he takes over the goods and the time of delivery”【CMR-17-1, cmr】.  

However, Article 17 also provides **exceptions** that relieve the carrier of liability. Paragraph 4(b) specifically lists “the lack of, or defective condition of packing in the case of goods which, by their nature, are liable to wastage or to be damaged when not packed or when not properly packed” as a circumstance where the carrier may be exempt from responsibility【CMR-17-4b, cmr】.  

Therefore, if the goods were inadequately packed by the shipper for a CIP sea journey (and the same situation were to occur on a road carriage governed by CMR), the damage would be attributed to the **defective packing**. Under the CMR rule, the carrier can be relieved of liability for that damage, and the **shipper** would bear the responsibility.  

In short, CMR makes the carrier liable **unless** the loss stems from defective packing; in that case the shipper is liable.
